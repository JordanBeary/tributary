# Problem framing: from business question to data science problem

Status: v1.1, 2026-09-02 (Round 2 of the 2026-08-31 repositioning, D14 accepted; rows 5 and 5b rewritten after the D11 acceptance pushback: "what is the point of each model")
Provenance: A — drafted by the agent from the built artifacts; the framing discipline is the author's (P-002 brief, 2026-08-31 direction)

The case study's primary claim is that a business question can be turned into a problem data science can answer, and that the answer can be turned back into a decision. This table makes that translation explicit for every question the project takes on. Each row states the decision at stake, the metric that would move, the counterfactual the analysis needs, the data that had to exist, the method chosen and why, the artifact that answers it, and how far the answer is from decision-grade (a recommendation with expected value, uncertainty, and a rollout plan).

Numbers cited here are recomputed by `analysis/dashboards/derived_figures.py`, `models/experiment_power.py`, and `models/validate_floors.py` from the committed aggregates and metrics; the site's overview renders a shortened form.

## How to read the status column

- **Answered** means a number with a chart exists and its inputs are traceable.
- **Modeled** means a model or counterfactual exists with a baseline it beats and its failure modes documented.
- **Decision-grade** means the artifact states a recommendation, its expected value, its uncertainty, the risks, and a rollout or test plan. Only rows 3 and 4 reach it.
- Row 5b is included deliberately as the counter-example: a well-built model attached to no decision. Keeping it visible is more useful than deleting it, because the failure mode it illustrates — a strong metric on a question nobody acts on — is common and hard to see from inside a model's own evaluation.
